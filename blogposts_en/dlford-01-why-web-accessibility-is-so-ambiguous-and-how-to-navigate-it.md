# Why Web Accessibility is so Ambiguous, and How to Navigate it

> **Source:** [dlford.io](https://www.dlford.io/web-accessibility-ambiguous/)  
> **Date:** February 14, 2026  
> **Tag:** `homelab`

<p></p>&#xA;<img&#xA; alt=&#34;An abstract digital infographic illustrating the transition from ambiguity to clarity in web accessibility. The left side features tangled, diverging arrows and question marks shrouded in a dark mist, representing confusion. The right side shows organized, glowing circuit paths leading to icons of a checklist, gears, and a person using assistive technology. At the center, a browser window displays the universal accessibility symbol, connecting the two halves on a dark, high-tech background.&#34;&#xA; &#xA; &#xA; width=&#34;600&#34;&#xA; height=&#34;335&#34;&#xA; src=&#34;https://www.dlford.io/media/rss/web-accessibility-ambiguous.4d1c78400ea69ed0b5dbc7fe639702a74463891e7cdf5e2a16b783ce24cf1f18_hu_4762c3ec61e8cb2c.png&#34;&#xA;/>&#xA;<p></p><h2 id=&#34;introduction&#34;>Introduction</h2>&#xA;<p>I would hope by now we&rsquo;re all well aware of web accessibility, at least in a big picture sense, but I think we could all benefit from a deeper understanding of what it means and how it is achieved. The intent of this article is to put anyone willing on the right path to becoming an expert on web accessibility, so let&rsquo;s start out by discussing why it matters so much.</p>&#xA;<h2 id=&#34;why-it-matters&#34;>Why It Matters</h2>&#xA;<p>Accessibility (often abbreviated as &ldquo;a11y&rdquo;) is both a moral and legal obligation. In much the same way that wheelchair access is both morally and legally required in many locations. Regulations vary by country and will change over time, a lot of these regulations are still pretty new and aren&rsquo;t being enforced yet, but the real motivation should be our moral obligation. The internet, one of the most powerful tools of the modern age, is for everyone, can you imagine being unable to use it?</p>&#xA;<p>Beyond the legal and moral implications, failure to address accessibility can have a financial impact on a business directly. Imagine you&rsquo;re trying to sign up for a service but their website isn&rsquo;t working correctly, how hard would you try to do business with this company before moving on to a competitor? Especially one that has a working website? What would you have to say if someone asked you about this company? This is the perspective of a person effected by inaccessibility.</p>&#xA;<h2 id=&#34;accounting-for-everyone&#34;>Accounting For Everyone</h2>&#xA;<p>We often think of a disability as being unable to see or hear, but these are only a few possible impairments a person could have. Hearing impairments can affect certain frequencies or ranges of frequencies, and visual impairments can affect only specific colors, or specific areas of the visual field, or even certain kinds of motion or flashing lights. We can&rsquo;t simply assume that a person with a visual impairment will use a screen reader and keyboard and completely stop using their monitor and mouse, they may choose to use any combination of those options. In this case, for example, over-use of &ldquo;screen reader only&rdquo; elements can make the site difficult to navigate and confusing for a person that uses both a screen reader and a monitor.</p>&#xA;<p>There are many other kinds of disabilities too, and not all of them permanent. Have you ever had a headache? Or broken a bone? Those are temporary disabilities that could impact how you interact with the internet.</p>&#xA;<p>This is not to say you need to be an expert on disabilities, rather that you should avoid making assumptions or broad generalizations. We cannot predict how any person will interact with a website, we must give them options, and ensure that any chosen combination of those options work together to provide a cohesive experience.</p>&#xA;<h2 id=&#34;the-web-content-accessibility-guidelines&#34;>The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines</h2>&#xA;<p>The <a&#xA; href=&#34;https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/&#34; target=&#34;_blank&#34; rel=&#34;noopener noreferrer&#34;>Web Content Accessibility Guidelines</a>&#xA; (or WCAG, for short) is a versioned collection of guidelines for web accessibility. This is an incredibly valuable tool, but it requires some interpretation to use, these are in fact guidelines rather than clear concise rules. At the core of WCAG there are four principles:</p>&#xA;<ol>&#xA;<li>Information and user interface components must be presentable to users in ways they can perceive.</li>&#xA;<li>User interface components and navigation must be operable.</li>&#xA;<li>Information and the operation of the user interface must be understandable.</li>&#xA;<li>Content must be robust enough that it can be interpreted by a wide variety of user agents, including assistive technologies.</li>&#xA;</ol>&#xA;<p>The rest of the guidelines go on to clarify and qualify what each of these principles means in specific contexts and situations. I highly recommend reading the entire thing at least once, but it is more of a reference piece. WCAG won&rsquo;t necessarily tell you how to make something accessible, but it will tell you how to measure accessibility.</p>&#xA;<p>There are three levels of conformance in the guidelines for WCAG 2: level A (minimum), level AA (standard), and level AAA (highest). Level AA includes everything in level A, and level AAA includes everything from all levels. Many regulations are targeting level AA as a minimum, but this is all going to change when WCAG 3 is finalized.</p>&#xA;<figure class=&#34;blockquote&#34;>&#xA; <blockquote cite=&#34;https://www.w3.org/TR/wcag-3.0/#about-wcag-3-0&#34;>&#xA; <p>Content that conforms to WCAG 2.2 Level A and Level AA is expected to meet most of the minimum conformance level of this new standard but, since WCAG 3.0 includes additional tests and different scoring mechanics, additional work will be needed to reach full conformance. Since the new standard will use a different conformance model, the Accessibility Guidelines Working Group expects that some organizations may wish to continue using WCAG 2, while others may wish to migrate to the new standard. For those that wish to migrate to WCAG 3, the Working Group will provide transition support materials, which may use mapping and other approaches to facilitate migration.</p>&#xA; </blockquote>&#xA; &#xA; <figcaption class=&#34;blockquote-caption&#34;>&#xA; <a href=&#34;https://www.w3.org/TR/wcag-3.0/#about-wcag-3-0&#34; target=&#34;_blank&#34; rel=&#34;noopener noreferrer nofollow&#34;>WCAG 3.0 Draft</a>&#xA; </figcaption>&#xA; &#xA;</figure><p>The guidelines also don&rsquo;t cover every possible situation, sometimes you have to evaluate a component on your own. In this case, all you have are the four principles, and it is up to you to interpret how they can be applied.</p>&#xA;<p>Ultimately, WCAG is a tool for measurement, finding missed opportunities, and fine tuning; but it should not be the <em>only</em> tool you reach for.</p>&#xA;<h2 id=&#34;other-tools-for-accessibility-evaluation&#34;>Other Tools for Accessibility Evaluation</h2>&#xA;<p>First and foremost, you <em>need</em> a screen reader, this is critical. For Windows I recommend <a&#xA; href=&#34;https://www.nvaccess.org/download/&#34; target=&#34;_blank&#34; rel=&#34;noopener noreferrer&#34;>NV Access NVDA Screen Reader</a>&#xA; to start out with (it&rsquo;s free! But you can donate if you&rsquo;d like), and for Linux you may already have <a&#xA; href=&#34;https://orca.gnome.org/&#34; target=&#34;_blank&#34; rel=&#34;noopener noreferrer&#34;>Orca</a>&#xA; ready to go. Since there are so many options and not all of them work the same, you might also want to test with more than one for good measure.</p>&#xA;<p>Browser developer tools have a growing selection of accessibility tools, for example in Chrome developer tools, under the hamburger menu, you will find a &ldquo;Rendering&rdquo; sub-menu with a plethora of options for emulating features like the <code>prefers-color-scheme</code> flag, and emulation of visual impairments (pro tip: use the <code>ctrl+shift+p</code> hotkey in Chrome dev tools and type &ldquo;emulate&rdquo; to get at these settings quickly). Under the &ldquo;Elements&rdquo; tab, you will find a button for switching to accessibility tree view, which emulates a screen reader menu visually. Under the &ldquo;Lighthouse&rdquo; tab there is an accessibility audit (keep in mind that this and other automatic tools like Axe are great for catching the low-hanging fruit, but are far from perfect and won&rsquo;t catch a lot of issues). There is a great <a&#xA; href=&#34;https://developer.chrome.com/docs/devtools/accessibility/reference&#34; target=&#34;_blank&#34; rel=&#34;noopener noreferrer&#34;>article here</a>&#xA; that covers these and other accessibility features of Chrome.</p>&#xA;<h2 id=&#34;working-with-assistive-technologies&#34;>Working with Assistive Technologies</h2>&#xA;<p>Screen readers and other assistive technologies do the best they can to gather context and meaning from the HTML on a web page, but there is a lot we can do to help them out.</p>&#xA;<p>Using proper <a&#xA; href=&#34;https://web.dev/learn/html/semantic-html&#34; target=&#34;_blank&#34; rel=&#34;noopener noreferrer&#34;>semantic HTML</a>&#xA; elements goes a long way to this end, giving the accessibility tree a more navigable and understandable structure. We should all be giving images proper alternative text by now, but it is worth mention here as well.</p>&#xA;<p>Choose HTML elements by their functionality rather than their appearance. Heading tags are an obvious one that tend to get abused for style, causing issues by confusing the structure of the document. We can take this even further though, for example if you were tasked with building a button group that can only have one active at a time; you should use radio inputs in a field set and style them with CSS instead of using the button element and manually add functionality with JavaScript. This way you&rsquo;re letting the browser take care of the accessibility for that element, and whenever the browser improves the way radio groups work you get those updates for free.</p>&#xA;<p>You should always reach for the browser supported features when you can, but what do you do when you can&rsquo;t? <a&#xA; href=&#34;https://www.w3.org/TR/wai-aria/#introduction&#34; target=&#34;_blank&#34; rel=&#34;noopener noreferrer&#34;>WAI-ARIA</a>&#xA; let&rsquo;s you add semantics to elements that don&rsquo;t have them by default, in the form of roles and states. I would recommend reading about each of the roles to have an idea of what is available, but this is mostly something you will mostly just reference as needed when you come across it.</p>&#xA;<p>Lastly, you&rsquo;ll need to use JavaScript to handle things like keyboard navigation when building custom widgets. It&rsquo;s important to stick to <a&#xA; href=&#34;https://govtnz.github.io/web-a11y-guidance/ka/accessible-ux-best-practices/keyboard-a11y/common-keyboard-commands.html&#34; target=&#34;_blank&#34; rel=&#34;noopener noreferrer&#34;>known conventions</a>&#xA; for keyboard controls to keep things predictable.</p>&#xA;<h2 id=&#34;manual-testing-is-the-key&#34;>Manual Testing is the Key</h2>&#xA;<p>There are no automated tools for testing web accessibility completely, given all the complexities involved this is not likely to change anytime soon. Additionally, sometimes assistive technologies can be unpredictable when working with custom widgets, recently added HTML or browser functions, and other unique scenarios.</p>&#xA;<p>The silver lining here is that manual testing creates a quick feedback loop for you to learn and grow your skills. You should manually test every component you build, at least until you can accurately predict issues before testing, over time you&rsquo;ll develop an intuition for what just works out of the box and when to suspect something might need additional work.</p>&#xA;<h2 id=&#34;final-thoughts&#34;>Final Thoughts</h2>&#xA;<p>In summary; web accessibility is a practice, not a feature, not a ticket or work item, and not a box you can check once and forget about. Accessibility requires consideration at every step of development, from building an MVP, to fixing a bug, to adding new features. Assistive technologies, browser features, and accessibility tools and methods are constantly evolving and improving, so make sure you keep up on new developments while you hone your skills.</p>&#xA;<p>If you have any questions along the way, or have anything to add to the discussion, please feel free to reach out in the comments below!</p>&#xA;
